    Syllabus — ECEG-6303 Advanced Digital Signal Processing¶

    MSc in Communication Engineering · Year 1, Semester I · Compulsory Core

    Student workload¶

    Lecture Tutorial Practice / Laboratory Home study
    2 h – 3 h 5 h

    Total per week: ~10 hours. Over 16 teaching weeks: ~160 hours, consistent with 5 ECTS.

    Note on tutorial hours. ECEG-6303 has no scheduled tutorial (unlike ECEG-6423). The 3-hour laboratory each week substitutes for a tutorial: the lab session begins with a short problem-solving segment covering the current chapter's worked examples, followed by the scheduled hands-on lab.

    Course description¶

    This course extends the classical digital signal processing sequence into statistical and adaptive signal processing. It develops the mathematical foundation for analysing random signals in discrete time, for estimating their spectra from finite data, for predicting and filtering them optimally, and for adapting filter coefficients to track changing statistics.

    Three threads run through the course.

    1. Statistical. Random signals, correlations, spectra, and how to estimate them from data.
    2. Estimation and prediction. ML and MMSE criteria, Wiener and Kalman filters, linear prediction, Levinson recursion, lattice filters.
    3. Adaptation. LMS, RLS, and their applications in equalisation, echo cancellation, and noise cancellation — plus the multirate tools (polyphase, filter banks) that make modern DSP hardware efficient.

    The course is the first of two DSP courses in the MSc programme. Its companion, ECEG-6423 Multimedia Signal Processing, applies these tools to images, speech, and video. ECEG-6303 is a prerequisite for the more advanced parts of ECEG-6423.

    Course objectives and competences¶

    Objectives¶

    On successful completion of this course, the student will be able to:

    • Comprehend advanced signal-processing techniques.
    • Carry out spectrum estimation and prediction.
    • Design adaptive filters.
    • Carry out multirate digital signal processing.

    Competences to be acquired¶

    • Apply digital signal-processing algorithms and design digital signal processors such as adaptive digital filters.

    Learning outcomes¶

    On successful completion, students will be able to:

    # Learning outcome Covered in Assessed by
    LO1 Analyse discrete random processes: expectation, variance, covariance, energy, Parseval's theorem, Wiener–Khinchine, PSD, periodogram, sample autocorrelation, Sum Decomposition, spectral factorization Ch 1 Test 1, Final
    LO2 Compute the output statistics of an LTI system driven by a WSS process; simulate white noise; simulate coloured processes by filtering white noise Ch 1 Lab 1, Final
    LO3 Estimate spectra using nonparametric (periodogram, Bartlett, Welch) and parametric (AR, MA, ARMA) methods; analyse bias and variance of each Ch 2 Test 2, Lab 2, Final
    LO4 Solve the Yule–Walker equations using Levinson–Durbin; select model order via AIC/MDL/FPE Ch 2 Lab 2, Final
    LO5 Apply the maximum likelihood and MMSE criteria; derive and use the Wiener filter; derive and use the Kalman filter; derive linear predictors Ch 3 Test 2, Lab 3, Final
    LO6 Design a Lattice filter and a Levinson-based predictor; derive the prediction-error filter as a whitening filter Ch 3 Lab 3, Final
    LO7 Design LMS and NLMS adaptive filters; state convergence conditions, time constants, and misadjustment; apply to equalisation, echo cancellation, noise cancellation Ch 4 Test 3, Lab 4, Final
    LO8 Derive and implement RLS (exponentially weighted and sliding window); compare RLS and LMS Ch 4 Lab 4, Final
    LO9 Apply decimation, interpolation, and polyphase decomposition; design a rational \(L/M\) resampler; state Noble identities Ch 5 Test 3, Lab 5, Final
    LO10 Design a two-channel QMF filter bank; state perfect-reconstruction conditions; describe the DWT as iterated filter banks Ch 5 Lab 5, Project, Final

    Teaching and learning methods¶

    • Lectures (2 h/week). Motivate and derive each topic; work through at least one example per method; demonstrate algorithms on the laptop.
    • Laboratory (3 h/week). Hands-on work in Python / MATLAB: implementing each chapter's core algorithms on synthetic and real data, measuring empirical bias/variance, convergence rates, and steady-state behaviour against theory.
    • Project (weeks 10–15). A self-contained implementation of one algorithm family (LMS, RLS, spectrum estimation, or filter banks) applied to a real dataset, with report and code.
    • Independent study (5 h/week). Reading, tutorial problems, lab prep, project work.

    Primary language: Python. MATLAB code is provided alongside for students whose lab is MATLAB-based.

    Chapter-by-chapter content¶

    Chapter 1 — Discrete Random Signal Processing¶

    Topics. Discrete random processes; expectation, variance, covariance; scalar product and energy of discrete signals; Parseval's theorem; Wiener–Khinchine relation; power spectral density; periodogram; sample autocorrelation; Sum Decomposition Theorem; Spectral Factorization Theorem; discrete random signal processing by linear systems; simulation of white noise; lowpass filtering of white noise.

    Key outcomes. LO1, LO2. Delivered week 1–4. Assessed in Test 1 and the final examination.

    Chapter 2 — Spectrum Estimation¶

    Topics. Nonparametric methods: correlation method, covariance estimator, performance analysis of estimators, unbiased and consistent estimators, periodogram, Bartlett spectrum estimation, Welch estimation. Model-based approach: AR, MA, ARMA signal modelling, parameter estimation using the Yule–Walker method, Levinson–Durbin recursion, model-order selection (AIC, MDL, FPE).

    Key outcomes. LO3, LO4. Delivered week 5–7. Assessed in Test 2, Lab 2, and the final.

    Chapter 3 — Linear Estimation and Prediction¶

    Topics. Maximum likelihood criterion; efficiency of estimators; least-mean-squared-error criterion; Wiener filter; discrete Wiener–Hopf equations; recursive estimators; Kalman filter; linear prediction; prediction error; whitening filter; inverse filter; Levinson recursion; lattice realization; Levinson recursion for solving Toeplitz systems.

    Key outcomes. LO5, LO6. Delivered week 8–10. Assessed in Test 2, Lab 3, and the final.

    Chapter 4 — Adaptive Filters¶

    Topics. FIR adaptive filters; Newton's steepest-descent method; adaptive filters based on steepest descent; Widrow–Hoff LMS adaptive algorithm; adaptive channel equalization; adaptive echo canceller; adaptive noise cancellation; RLS adaptive filters; exponentially weighted RLS; sliding-window RLS; simplified IIR LMS adaptive filter; NLMS.

    Key outcomes. LO7, LO8. Delivered week 11–13. Assessed in Test 3, Lab 4, and the final.

    Chapter 5 — Multirate Digital Signal Processing¶

    Topics. Mathematical description of sampling-rate change; interpolation and decimation; continuous-time model; direct digital-domain approach; decimation by integer factor; interpolation by integer factor; single and multistage realization; polyphase realization; Noble identities; applications to subband coding; wavelet transform and filter-bank implementation of wavelet expansion of signals; two-channel QMF filter banks.

    Key outcomes. LO9, LO10. Delivered week 14–16. Assessed in Test 3, Lab 5, the project, and the final.

    Course policy¶

    • All students are expected to abide by the Code of Conduct for Students in the Senate Legislation of the University throughout this course.
    • 80% lecture attendance is mandatory.
    • 100% laboratory attendance is mandatory. Missing more than one lab without documented cause results in loss of the lab-report mark for the affected session.
    • Academic integrity. All submitted work must be your own. Plagiarism and unauthorised AI-generated work result in a zero on the affected component and a report to the Dean's office. See the Assessment page for the full academic-integrity policy.
