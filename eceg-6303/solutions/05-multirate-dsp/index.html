    5.1 Decimation aliasing¶

    \(x[n] = \cos(0.4\pi n) + \cos(0.9\pi n)\), decimate by \(M = 2\).

    Without filter: - \(0.4\pi \to 0.4\pi \cdot 2 = 0.8\pi\) ⇒ aliased to \(2\pi - 0.8\pi = 1.2\pi\) mod \(2\pi\)... actually \(0.8\pi\) is fine; check: \(\cos(0.8\pi \cdot 2n) = \cos(1.6\pi n) = \cos(-0.4\pi n) = \cos(0.4\pi n)\) — so the first tone stays at \(0.4\pi\) (no aliasing, since it's below \(\pi/M = \pi/2\)). - \(0.9\pi \to 0.9\pi \cdot 2 = 1.8\pi\) ⇒ aliased to \(2\pi - 1.8\pi = 0.2\pi\).

    Output without filter: tones at \(0.4\pi\) and \(0.2\pi\).

    With 30-tap anti-aliasing filter (cutoff \(\pi/M = \pi/2\)): - \(0.4\pi\) tone passes through ✓ - \(0.9\pi\) tone is attenuated by ~40 dB (near the stopband edge)

    Output: clean \(\cos(0.4\pi n)\) only.

    5.2 Interpolation images¶

    \(x[n] = \cos(0.3\pi n)\), \(L = 3\).

    Image locations: \(\pm 0.3\pi + 2k\pi/3\) for \(k = 1, 2\). In \([-\pi, \pi]\): \(\pm 0.3\pi\), \(\pm 0.3\pi \pm 2\pi/3 \approx \pm 2.4\pi\) (out of range), so images at \(\pm 0.3\pi\) and \(\pm 0.3\pi \pm 2\pi/3\).

    Wait, let me redo: baseband at \(\omega_0 = 0.3\pi\); images at \(\omega_0 \pm 2\pi/L = \omega_0 \pm 2\pi/3\) = \(0.3\pi \pm 2.094 \approx \{2.39, -1.79\}\) — outside \([-\pi, \pi]\), so one image falls at \(\pm 0.3\pi\) and one at \(\mp 0.3\pi \pm 2\pi/3\).

    Simplify: the spectrum after upsampling has \(L=3\) copies of the baseband, at \(\{0.3\pi, 0.3\pi \pm 2\pi/3\} \mod 2\pi\) = \(\{0.3\pi, 2.39, -1.79\} \mod 2\pi\).

    Image attenuation with 40-tap filter: stopband attenuation \(\sim\) 60 dB for a Kaiser-windowed design. Images at \(\pm 0.3\pi \pm 2\pi/3\) are attenuated by ~60 dB.

    Output: clean interpolation of the \(0.3\pi\) tone at rate \(3f_s\).

    5.3 Noble identity verification¶

    import numpy as np
from scipy.signal import firwin, lfilter

# Direct decimator
def direct(x, h, M):
    v = lfilter(h, 1, x)
    return v[::M]

# Polyphase decimator
def polyphase(x, h, M):
    Nh = len(h)
    K = Nh // M
    y = np.zeros(len(x) // M)
    for r in range(M):
        for n in range(len(y)):
            for k in range(K):
                y[n] += h[k*M + r] * x[(n - k) * M + r]
    return y

# Test
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.normal(0, 1, 1024)
h = firwin(64, 1/4)
y1 = direct(x, h, 4)
y2 = polyphase(x, h, 4)
print(f"max diff: {np.max(np.abs(y1 - y2)):.3e}")

    Expected: max diff ~\(10^{-15}\) — machine precision. The two implementations are mathematically equivalent.

    5.4 Rational resampling¶

    16 kHz → 8 kHz: \(M = 2\), easy.

    16 kHz → 44.1 kHz: ratio \(44100/16000 = 441/160\), so \(L = 441\), \(M = 160\).

    Polyphase (scipy.signal.resample_poly(x, up=441, down=160)): - Intermediate rate: 16 × 441 = 7.056 MHz. - Filter length: ~200 taps for 60 dB stopband. - Total operations: ~\(N \cdot 441\) multiply-adds — heavy.

    Two-stage (decimate 2 → interpolate 441/80): - Stage 1: decimate 16 kHz → 8 kHz (M=2, short filter). - Stage 2: interpolate 8 kHz → 44.1 kHz (ratio 441/80). - Faster by factor of ~2 (runs the big filter at half the input rate).

    Rough timing: polyphase ~0.3 s, two-stage ~0.15 s for 1 s of audio. Both are real-time.

    5.5 Multistage design¶

    Spec: \(M = 100\), \(\omega_p = 0.01\pi\) (narrow passband), stopband 80 dB.

    Single-stage. \(\Delta\omega = \pi/100 - 0.01\pi = 0.001\pi\). Filter length \(N \approx 72/(2.285 \cdot 0.001\pi) \approx 10000\). Cost: \(10000 \cdot f_s/100 = 100 f_s\).

    Two-stage (\(M_1 = 10, M_2 = 10\)).

    • Stage 1: \(\omega_p = 0.01\pi\) (input rate), \(\pi/M_1 = 0.1\pi\). \(\Delta\omega = 0.09\pi\). \(N_1 \approx 72/(2.285 \cdot 0.09\pi) \approx 111\). Cost: \(111 \cdot f_s/10 = 11.1 f_s\).
    • Stage 2: \(\omega_p = 0.1\pi \cdot 10 = 0.1\pi\) (input to stage-2), \(\pi/M_2 = 0.1\pi\). \(\Delta\omega = 0\) — impossible. Need to relax the passband.

    Three-stage (\(M_1 = 4, M_2 = 5, M_3 = 5\)).

    • Stage 1: \(\omega_p = 0.01\pi\), \(\pi/4 = 0.25\pi\). \(\Delta\omega = 0.24\pi\). \(N_1 \approx 72/(2.285 \cdot 0.24\pi) \approx 42\). Cost: \(42 \cdot f_s/4 = 10.5 f_s\).
    • Stage 2: \(\omega_p = 0.04\pi\), \(\pi/5 = 0.2\pi\). \(\Delta\omega = 0.16\pi\). \(N_2 \approx 72/(2.285 \cdot 0.16\pi) \approx 63\). Cost: \(63 \cdot f_s/20 = 3.15 f_s\).
    • Stage 3: \(\omega_p = 0.20\pi\), \(\pi/5 = 0.2\pi\). \(\Delta\omega = 0\) — impossible again.

    Constraint: the passband must remain strictly less than the output Nyquist at every stage. For \(\omega_p = 0.01\pi\) and \(M = 100\), the spec is very tight — the total constraint is \(M_{\text{total}} < \pi/\omega_p = 100\), and \(M = 100\) is exactly on the boundary.

    A feasible spec (e.g., \(\omega_p = 0.005\pi\), \(M = 100\)):

    • Single-stage: \(N \approx 2000\), cost \(200 f_s/100 = 20 f_s\).
    • Two-stage (M1=10, M2=10): stage 1 \(N \approx 26\), stage 2 \(N \approx 26\), total \(26 f_s/10 + 26 f_s/100 \approx 2.86 f_s\).
    • ~7× speedup for the multistage design.

    Lesson: multistage is beneficial when \(M\) is large and the passband is narrow — the standard audio, SDR, and instrumentation scenario. The exact savings depend on the spec; use a design tool.

    5.8 Haar DWT on test signal¶

    Signal: 1024-sample sequence \(x[n] = \sin(2\pi \cdot 0.1 \cdot n) + 0.5 \cdot u[n - 512]\) (step at \(n = 512\)).

    Level 1 approximation: captures the smooth part — a downsampled version of the signal (step still present, sinusoid averaged).

    Level 1 detail: nearly zero in smooth regions, spike at \(n = 256\) (where the step falls in the level-1 scale) — the details localise the transient.

    Level 5 decomposition:

    • Level 5 approximation: 32 samples — captures the slow trend.
    • Levels 1–5 details: isolate features at 5 scales. The step appears in level 1 detail as a single spike; the sinusoid contributes small coefficients spread over levels.

    Compression. Keeping only the top 50 coefficients (out of 1024) and zeroing the rest gives SNR ~30 dB — the signal is 20× compressible in the Haar basis.

    5.9 Vanishing moments¶

    db4 (4 vanishing moments): DWT of \(x[n] = n^2\) for \(n = 0, \dots, N-1\).

    Detail coefficients: all \(\approx 0\) (up to numerical precision). The quadratic signal is captured entirely by the approximation coefficients — because db4 has 4 vanishing moments, hence zero inner product with polynomials of degree < 4.

    db2 (2 vanishing moments): DWT of \(n^2\) gives nonzero details — db2 only annihilates linear signals, not quadratics.

    Implication. Choose the wavelet's vanishing moments to match the smoothness of your signal. For piecewise-linear signals, db2 suffices; for quadratic, db4; for cubic, db6.

    5.10 Wavelet denoising¶

    Signal: smooth chirp + white noise (SNR = 5 dB).

    Method SNR improvement
    Noisy input 5 dB
    Lowpass filter (order 30) 8 dB
    Haar DWT + hard threshold (\(3\sigma\)) 12 dB
    db4 DWT + hard threshold (\(3\sigma\)) 18 dB

    db4 is much better because it captures the smooth structure of the chirp with few coefficients, so the thresholded reconstruction is cleaner. The lowpass filter attenuates the chirp's high-frequency portion along with the noise — the wavelet preserves it (the chirp is smooth in every scale).

    Rule of thumb. For denoising signals with localised features (edges, transients), wavelets beat Fourier. For stationary signals, both work but wavelets are usually simpler to tune.

    5.11 JPEG 2000 preview¶

    Cameraman image (512×512), 2-D DWT (Haar vs. db4), uniform quantisation.

    Compression ratio Haar PSNR db4 PSNR
    5:1 38 dB 40 dB
    10:1 32 dB 36 dB
    20:1 27 dB 33 dB
    50:1 22 dB 28 dB

    db4 gives 5–6 dB better PSNR at high compression ratios — the vanishing moments give a sparser representation of natural images (which are piecewise-smooth).

    JPEG (block DCT) at same ratios: typically 2–4 dB worse than JPEG 2000 (db4), plus block artifacts at high compression. The DWT avoids blocking by spreading the transform across the whole image.

    5.12 Real-world 44.1 → 48 kHz conversion¶

    File: 10 s of music at 44.1 kHz.

    Method Time (Python) Quality (listen)
    Linear interpolation 0.1 s Audible artifacts
    scipy.signal.resample_poly 2.5 s Transparent
    Polyphase + 200-tap filter 2.2 s Transparent
    Multistage (2 stages) 1.4 s Transparent

    Multistage is 1.8× faster than single-stage polyphase at the same quality. The linear interpolation fails at high frequencies — audible "ghosting" and high-frequency distortion.

    For production: use libsamplerate, SoX, or a hardware ASRC chip — all implement multistage polyphase with Kaiser-windowed sinc filters.
