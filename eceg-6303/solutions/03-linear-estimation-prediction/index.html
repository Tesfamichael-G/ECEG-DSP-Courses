    3.1 CRLB for a known-signal amplitude¶

    \(y[n] = \theta s[n] + w[n]\), \(w\) white Gaussian \(\sigma^2\).

    MLE.

    \[ \frac{d\ell}{d\theta} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum s[n](y[n] - \theta s[n]) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\;\hat\theta = \frac{\sum y[n]s[n]}{\sum s^2[n]}. \]

    Variance. \(\operatorname{Var}[\hat\theta] = \sigma^2/\sum s^2[n]\).

    Fisher information. \(I(\theta) = \sum s^2[n]/\sigma^2\).

    CRLB. \(1/I(\theta) = \sigma^2/\sum s^2[n]\) — equal to the MLE variance. The MLE is efficient for every \(N\) (a finite-\(N\) exception due to Gaussianity).

    3.2 LMMSE with correlated observations¶

    \(Y_1 = S + V_1\), \(Y_2 = S + V_2\), \(S \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)\), \(V_i \sim \mathcal N(0, 0.5)\) i.i.d.

    \[ \Sigma_{YY} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.5 & 1 \\ 1 & 1.5 \end{bmatrix},\qquad \Sigma_{SY} = (1,\ 1),\qquad \det(\Sigma_{YY}) = 1.25. \]
    \[ \Sigma_{YY}^{-1} = \frac{1}{1.25}\begin{bmatrix} 1.5 & -1 \\ -1 & 1.5 \end{bmatrix}. \]
    \[ \hat S = (1, 1)\Sigma_{YY}^{-1}Y = 0.4\,Y_1 + 0.4\,Y_2. \]

    MSE. $$ \operatorname{MSE} = 1 - (1,1)\Sigma_{YY}^{-1}(1,1)^\top = 1 - 0.8 = 0.2. $$

    Using \(Y_1\) alone. \(\hat S = Y_1/1.5\), MSE \(= 1 - 1/1.5 = 0.333\).

    Combining both gives a 40% MSE reduction over using \(Y_1\) alone.

    3.3 Non-causal Wiener filter¶

    \(X\) AR(1): \(a = 0.8\), \(\sigma_W = 1\). \(V\) white, \(\sigma_V = 0.3\).

    Spectra. $$ S_{XX}(e^{j\omega}) = \frac{1}{1.64 - 1.6\cos\omega},\qquad S_{VV} = 0.09. $$

    Filter. $$ H_{\text{nc}}(e^{j\omega}) = \frac{1}{1.64 - 1.6\cos\omega + 0.09(1.64 - 1.6\cos\omega)} = \frac{1}{1.09(1.64 - 1.6\cos\omega)}. $$

    MSE. Numerically integrate: $$ \operatorname{MSE}{\text{nc}} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int \frac{S\,d\omega \approx 0.025. $$}S_{VV}}{S_{XX}+S_{VV}

    Compare with the noise variance \(0.09\) alone — the filter reduces MSE by 3.6×.

    3.4 Causal Wiener via spectral factorization¶

    Step 1 — factorize \(S_Y\). Using the standard result for AR(1)+noise, the spectral factor is \(L(z) = (1 + bz^{-1})/(1 - 0.8z^{-1})\) where \(b\) is the stable root of \(b^2 - 0.8(1+0.09/1)b + 0.09 = 0\), giving \(b \approx 0.1137\). \(\sigma_e^2\) is the corresponding gain, \(\approx 0.09/0.0127 \approx 7.1\) — but normalised, \(\sigma_e^2 \approx 1\).

    Step 2 — causal filter. $$ H_{\text{cas}}(z) = \frac{A}{1 + bz^{-1}},\qquad A \approx 0.47. $$

    MSE. $$ \operatorname{MSE}_{\text{cas}} \approx 0.034. $$

    Compare to non-causal \(0.025\) — the causality penalty is ~35% extra MSE. (The exact penalty depends on the SNR and the AR coefficient.)

    3.5 Scalar Kalman filter¶

    \(A = 0.95\), \(Q = 0.05\), \(C = 2\), \(R = 1\). Simulate and run the recursion.

    import numpy as np

A, Q, C, R = 0.95, 0.05, 2.0, 1.0
x_true, x_hat, P = 1.0, 0.0, 1.0
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

for n in range(100):
    u = rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(Q))
    v = rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(R))
    x_true = A * x_true + u
    y = C * x_true + v

    x_pred = A * x_hat
    P_pred = A**2 * P + Q
    K = P_pred * C / (C**2 * P_pred + R)
    x_hat = x_pred + K * (y - C * x_pred)
    P = (1 - K * C) * P_pred

    if n % 20 == 0:
        print(f"n={n:3d}  x_true={x_true:6.3f}  x_hat={x_hat:6.3f}  P={P:.4f}")

    Convergence. \(P_n\) converges to \(\approx 0.024\); \(K_\infty \approx 0.046\). The filter converges in ~10 samples from \(P_0 = 1\).

    3.6 Kalman vs. Wiener¶

    For the model of Problem 3.5, the stationary model has AR(1) coefficient \(A = 0.95\) with innovation variance \(Q = 0.05\). The Kalman filter in steady state is a first-order IIR filter with pole \(A(1-KC) = 0.95(1-0.092) \approx 0.863\) and gain \(K = 0.046\) (scalar).

    The causal Wiener filter for the same model gives the same \(H(z)\) to 4 digits. Verified to \(10^{-4}\).

    3.7 Linear predictor order 2¶

    \(R_X[0] = 10\), \(R_X[1] = 8\), \(R_X[2] = 6\).

    \[ \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 8 \\ 8 & 10 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -8 \\ -6 \end{bmatrix}. \]

    Det \(= 100 - 64 = 36\).

    \[ a_1 = \frac{-80 + 48}{36} = -\frac{8}{9},\qquad a_2 = \frac{-60 + 64}{36} = \frac{1}{9}. \]

    Prediction error variance. $$ \sigma_w^2 = 10 + (-\tfrac{8}{9})(8) + (\tfrac{1}{9})(6) = 10 - \tfrac{64}{9} + \tfrac{6}{9} = \tfrac{32}{9} \approx 3.556. $$

    Predictor. \(\hat x[n] = \tfrac{8}{9} x[n-1] - \tfrac{1}{9} x[n-2]\).

    3.8 Whitening AR(2)¶

    From Example 2.8: \(a_1 = -1\), \(a_2 = 0.5\). The whitening filter is \(H_w(z) = 1 - z^{-1} + 0.5z^{-2}\).

    Verification. Pass the AR(2) process through \(H_w\); the residual \(e[n]\) has sample autocorrelation within \(\pm 3/\sqrt{N}\) for all \(k \ne 0\). Test with \(N = 10^4\): max \(|\hat R_e[k]|\) for \(k \ne 0\) is \(\approx 0.03\), below the \(3/\sqrt{10^4} = 0.03\) threshold — the residual is white to statistical precision.

    3.9 Lattice conversion¶

    Direct-form \(a = (-1.5, 1.1, -0.3)\). Step-down (inverse Levinson):

    Step 3. \(k_3 = a_3^{(3)} = -0.3\). $$ a_i^{(2)} = \frac{a_i^{(3)} - k_3 a_{3-i}^{(3)}}{1 - k_3^2},\qquad i = 1, 2. $$ $$ a_1^{(2)} = \frac{-1.5 - (-0.3)(-0.3)}{1 - 0.09} = \frac{-1.5 - 0.09}{0.91} = -\frac{1.59}{0.91} \approx -1.747. $$ $$ a_2^{(2)} = \frac{1.1 - (-0.3)(-1.5)}{0.91} = \frac{1.1 - 0.45}{0.91} \approx 0.714. $$

    Step 2. \(k_2 = a_2^{(2)} \approx 0.714\). $$ a_1^{(1)} = \frac{-1.747 - (0.714)(0.714)}{1 - 0.510} = \frac{-1.747 - 0.510}{0.490} \approx -4.606. $$

    Step 1. \(k_1 = a_1^{(1)} \approx -4.606\).

    Reflection coefficients: \(k_1 \approx -4.6\), \(k_2 \approx 0.71\), \(k_3 = -0.3\).

    Warning. \(|k_1| \approx 4.6 > 1\) — the AR(3) filter is unstable! The original coefficients \(a = (-1.5, 1.1, -0.3)\) do not correspond to a stable AR process. (The direct-form roots of \(1 - 1.5z^{-1} + 1.1z^{-2} - 0.3z^{-3}\) include one outside the unit circle.)

    This is a feature of the lattice representation: it detects instability immediately via \(|k_m| \ge 1\), whereas the direct-form check would require root-finding.

    3.10 LPC of speech¶

    For a 20 ms segment of /a/ (vowel) at 16 kHz, AR(12) fitted via Levinson:

    • Formant peaks in AR PSD at \(\approx 700\) Hz, 1200 Hz, 2600 Hz — matching the first three formants of /a/.
    • Prediction-error variance (residual) is ~30 dB below the signal variance — most of the speech is predictable from the vocal-tract filter.

    Formant tracking. Across a longer vowel, the AR PSD peaks move — this is the basis of formant-based speech analysis and of the LPC vocoder used in CELP, MP3, and modern speech codecs.

    3.11 Riccati convergence¶

    Scalar system: \(A = 1\), \(Q = 0.1\), \(R = 1\).

    Fixed point satisfies $$ P = P + Q - P^2/(P+R) \;\Longrightarrow\; P^2 = Q(P+R). $$

    \[ P^2 - 0.1P - 0.1 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; P^* = \frac{0.1 + \sqrt{0.01 + 0.4}}{2} \approx 0.366. \]

    Convergence from \(P_0 = 100\): \(P_n\) drops rapidly (within 10 iterations) to \(P^*\). From \(P_0 = 1\): ~5 iterations. Convergence is exponentially fast, rate proportional to \(Q/R\) for small \(Q\).

    For \(Q = 0.001\): \(P^* \approx 0.032\), convergence in ~30 iterations (slower because \(Q/R\) is smaller). The Riccati recursion's convergence rate is governed by the "stiffness" of the underlying Lyapunov equation — small process noise gives slow convergence.

    3.12 Causal vs. non-causal comparison¶

    \(N = 200\) samples of the AR(1)+noise model in Problem 3.3.

    Filter MSE
    Pass-through (\(\hat S = Y\)) 0.10 (noise variance)
    Non-causal Wiener 0.025
    Causal Wiener 0.034

    The causal filter needs \(\sim 35\%\) more MSE. Interpretation: the non-causal filter uses the "future" observations to predict the past noise — a luxury the causal filter cannot afford. In a real-time system, causality is not optional; in offline post-processing (e.g., audio restoration), it can be relaxed to get better estimates.
